## When to use

- Drafting an email, Slack message, or any direct written communication to a real person or group.
- Writing a cover note for an attachment (deck, report, doc) where you want the attachment to do the work.
- Any comms where the default AI output would create distance between writer and reader.

This skill is NOT for: long-form articles, technical docs, or content where formality is the actual requirement.

## Method

### Step 1: Map the context

Before writing a word, answer three questions:

1. Who is receiving this, and what is their default emotional state when they open it?
2. What is the implicit pressure this message creates? (Decision required? Action expected? Judgment being made?)
3. What do you want them to do or feel after reading it?

These answers shape every word that follows.

### Step 2: Draft the subject or title

Two jobs: name the domain, signal the pressure level.

- Domain: tells the reader what this is before they open it.
- Pressure signal: sets the emotional stakes. "Food for thought" reads differently from "Action required."

If pressure is low: say so in the subject. The reader will relax before they start.

### Step 3: Strip the opener

Write the opener, then cut it until only the necessary word or phrase remains.

Kill on sight:
- "I hope this email finds you well"
- `"I wanted to reach out"`
- "I am writing to"
- "Dear [title]"

Replace with: "Hi [name/team]," or nothing at all.

### Step 4: Write the first sentence as action, not sender

The first sentence after the opener names what is happening, not who is writing.

Wrong: "I have put together a short deck that I wanted to share with you regarding..."
Right: "Attaching a short deck on where we are with AI adoption."

The sender is already known. Lead with the thing.

### Step 5: Add the short descriptor

Add one adjective before a noun that might create an objection. A short deck answers "do I have time for this?" before the reader asks. A quick question pre-empts "is this going to take long?"

Only use it when the objection is real. Not every noun needs one.

### Step 6: Pre-empt the implicit pressure

Find the implicit pressure your message creates. State explicitly what you are NOT asking for, before the reader feels it.

Pattern: start with the negation. "Not asking for a decision" lands before "here is my thinking." The relief arrives before the content.

This is not apologizing for sending the message. It is removing friction the reader would have spent mental energy on.

### Step 7: Own the opinion

Use "I think" for views. Not "it appears that," not "one could argue," not "the data suggests."

If you believe something, say you believe it. Hedged opinions read as either cowardice or AI.

### Step 8: Name the response you want

Tell the reader exactly what you want back. Name it specifically.

Wrong: "Let me know if you have any thoughts."
Right: "If anything in there sparks questions or pushback, I'd like to hear it."

Naming "pushback" signals you can handle disagreement. Generic "thoughts" invites nothing.

### Step 9: Write the closer as two words

Close with two words. Three at most.

- "Feedback welcome."
- "Let me know."
- "Over to you."

No "Kind regards." No `"Please don't hesitate to reach out."` No "Looking forward to your response."

The closer is not a social ritual. It is the last signal about tone. Keep it the same as the rest of the email: direct.

### Step 10: Run the distance audit

Read the full draft and find every word or phrase that creates distance. Distance language is anything that signals institution over person, process over thought, or formality over honesty.

Common distance patterns to cut:
- "Please find attached"
- "At your earliest convenience"
- "I wanted to take the opportunity to"
- "As per my previous"
- "Going forward"
- `"Touch base"`
- Any passive construction that hides the actor ("a decision has been made" vs "I decided")

Cut or rewrite each one before sending.

## Failure modes

- **Draft competes with the attachment**: if the email summarizes the doc, the reader skims the email and skips the doc. The email's job is to create pull toward the attachment, not replace it. Cut any sentence that restates what is already in the attached content.
- **Pre-emption sounds like an apology**: "Not asking for a decision" is neutral and practical. "Sorry to bother you" is an apology. The first removes friction. The second creates it by implying the message should not have been sent.
- **Closer is too abrupt for the relationship**: "Feedback welcome." works for colleagues. For a first contact or a senior stakeholder you have not met, use a single sentence closer. This is safer: "Happy to answer questions if anything needs more context." Calibrate to the actual relationship.
- **Two-word opener sounds cold**: "Hi team," is fine for groups. For a single person you know well, "Hi [name]," lands warmer without adding any distance language. Use the name.
